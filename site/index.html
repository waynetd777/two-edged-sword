Two-edged Sword

Bible study for Mac and Windows. Every commentary, lexicon and note you own, open at the verse you're reading.

Download for Mac Download for Windows Read the guide

Free software. Mac: Apple silicon, macOS 13 or later. Windows: 10 or 11, 64-bit.

New here? Take the tutorials ›

Read a chapter beside everything your library says about it.

Select a verse and the study pane beside it shows every commentary you own on it, its cross‑references, your dictionaries and your own notes. Click any word to see the Greek or Hebrew behind it.

  1. The chapter, and the study pane beside it
  2. Every commentary on the verse, in the order you choose
  3. Highlights in eight colours, each named for a theme you choose
  4. Related verses from the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge

Where a translation reads differently, and why.

A ≠ beside a verse marks where the translation differs in meaning from the King James, such as an omitted phrase or a changed name of God. Click it for both readings, what changed, and which manuscripts each follows.

The app comes with these notes for the NIV, ESV, ASV, CEV, GNB, MKJV (1998 and 1993), ISV and the Rheims New Testament of 1582, and for the built-in Berean Standard Bible, Revised Version, Darby, Weymouth, Douay-Rheims, Brenton's Septuagint, and Murdock's and Etheridge's English Peshitta, each checked across the whole Bible or New Testament. They show for whichever of these you have.

  1. Colossians 1 in the American Standard Version
  2. Verse 14 omits “through his blood”. Both readings, and the manuscripts behind each.

Greek, Hebrew, Latin and Syriac, word by word.

Each original word sits over its English and its Strong's number, the standard number for that word in Bible dictionaries. Hover a word for its grammar; click it to look it up. Hebrew and Syriac read right to left, as they should.

  1. Greek · Beza's New Testament, 1598
  2. Hebrew · the Leningrad Codex
  3. Syriac · the Peshitta

Compare. Search. Study a word.

Put translations side by side with the differences marked. Search a Bible, every commentary and dictionary, your books and your journal at once. Or gather everything about one Greek or Hebrew word on a single page.

  1. Compare
  2. Search
  3. Word Study

Any number of translations, verse by verse. Highlight differences marks the wording that differs from the first column.

Maps where every place leads to its verses.

Fifteen maps drawn for the app, from the patriarchs and the Exodus to Paul's journeys and the seven churches, with plans of Jerusalem in the Old Testament and in Jesus' day. The study pane shows the ones for the book you're reading.

Click a place to list every verse that names it; point to a verse to read it, or click it to go there. Open a map full screen to zoom in, and the places still work.

  1. The maps for the book you're reading
  2. Capernaum: every verse that names it, each a click away

Hear it read, word by word.

Chapters, books, devotionals and journal entries are read aloud, each word lit as it's spoken. It uses your computer's voices: Hebrew and Greek Bibles are read in a Hebrew or Greek voice, and Latin in an Italian one.

This then is the message which we have heard of him, and declare unto you, that God is light, and in him is no darkness at all. 1 John 1:5

Ask, and it answers from your own shelves.

Ask a question about the passage and the assistant reads your commentaries, lexicons and dictionaries to answer it, naming its source for each point, and saying when your library has nothing. It works through an AI tool you already have installed, with your own account.

  1. “How do the commentators differ on this verse?”
  2. Gill, Barnes, Clarke and Wesley, read and compared
  3. Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity or GitHub Copilot: whichever you have
  • Read only. The AI tool can only read and search, so it can't change your files.
  • Knows the guide. Ask how to do something in the app, and it answers from the help.
  • Respects licences. One switch stops any copyrighted Bible or book being sent with your questions.
  • Into your journal. Any answer can be added to an entry in one click.

Notes that know their verses.

Each entry is linked to the verses it's about, and shows beside them whenever you read them. References you type become links, even misspelled ones such as “Isiah 53”. Highlight and tag entries, and ask the assistant about one entry or the whole journal.

Print a month, a tag or every entry on a passage as a PDF: a cover showing where in the Bible you've been, the words and phrases you keep returning to, and, if you like, the assistant's overview of how the entries fit together.

Entries are plain-text Markdown files in your Documents folder. Keep them in a folder of a notes app that works with Markdown files, such as Brainstead, and changes made there show in the app within seconds.

A plan for the morning, and music to go with it.

Choose a reading plan: the Bible in a year, the New Testament in 90 days, a Psalm and a Proverb a day, or your own. Each day, Quiet time takes you through the readings, with worship songs from your music library that the assistant chooses to suit them, and ends with a verse that sums up the day.

  1. Today's readings, your streak, and the days ahead
  2. Songs chosen for today's reading, and why each suits it
  3. The words as they're sung, with a picture behind them in the colours of the song's artwork, and a verse beneath it
  4. A closing verse, and a few lines on how it sums up the day

In the menu bar all day.

Close the window and the app stays in the menu bar (on Windows, by the clock), showing today's reading, your streak and how many of the last seven days you've read. Start Quiet time from there, carry on where you left off, or begin a journal entry.

A daily reminder arrives at the time you choose, if you haven't read yet.

Songs for any chapter, too: click ♫ and the assistant picks worship songs to suit the chapter you're reading, in the Bible or in a book.

Where the King James came from, and what came after.

A family tree of the King James Bible: the manuscript traditions behind it, the printed editions on the translators' desks at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, and the English Bibles before it. Then the English Bibles since, by family: the KJV kept, the revision line from the Revised Version to the ESV, the new translations and the plain-English ones. Each card shows which texts are in your library and opens them; its ⓘ says what it gave the King James, or compares a later Bible with the one it was revised from.

  1. Before the KJV
  2. After the KJV

Comes with a library. Reads yours too.

Out of the box: the King James with Strong's numbers and some twenty more Bibles, among them the Berean Standard Bible, Tyndale, the Geneva, the Vulgate, the Septuagint, the Peshitta, and the Hebrew Old Testament of the Leningrad and Aleppo Codices and of Ginsburg's 1894 edition; Strong's Hebrew and Greek dictionaries, and the Brown-Driver-Briggs Hebrew and Abbott-Smith Greek lexicons; the Treasury of Scripture Knowledge, commentaries from Matthew Henry and Scofield to Keil & Delitzsch and Spurgeon's Treasury of David, dictionaries from Easton's to the ISBE, fifteen Bible maps drawn for the app, and a shelf of classic books, from Josephus, Edersheim and Strong's Systematic Theology to The Pilgrim's Progress and six of Larkin's books with their charts.

It also reads the Bibles, commentaries and books made for e‑Sword X, a long-standing free Bible app, and on a Mac reads e‑Sword X's own library if you have it. In books and devotionals you can highlight, bookmark, note, listen to or ask about any paragraph, as you would a verse, and their charts and drawings open full screen to zoom into. A Bible's own notes, such as the ESV's cross‑references and footnotes, show in the text.

Build more from free sources with the scripts in the source code: the Talmud and the Targums in English, Beza's Greek New Testament and the Rheims New Testament of 1582.

Two-edged Sword

For a Mac with Apple silicon running macOS 13 or later, or a PC running Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit).

Download for Mac Download for Windows View on GitHub

On a Mac

  1. Open the disk image and drag Two-edged Sword onto Applications.
  2. Open it once. macOS says it can't check the app for malware, because it isn't registered (notarised) with Apple. Click Done.
  3. In System Settings › Privacy & Security, click Open Anyway. It opens normally after that.

On Windows

  1. Run the installer. It installs for you alone, with no administrator password.
  2. If Windows says it protected your PC (the app isn't code-signed), click More info, then Run anyway.
  3. Open Two-edged Sword from the Start menu.

Once it's open, press ? on any screen for its help. Getting started, in the help, walks you through the app a step at a time.
